Chapter 1 · Where I walked in

Nobody comes in neutral

Where do I stand?

Dr. Meekings did not open with a claim. He opened with a drawing his daughter made, which he had promised to share, and then he named something I did not expect: nobody comes to the AI debate neutrally.

Some of us are keen to see what we can generate. Others are skeptical and pulled back toward older media. He pointed to print books outselling e-books again over the last decade, and to vinyl, once a bargain-bin format, being sought out again.

I liked that he named the feeling first. So here is mine. Pick yours.

Chapter 2 · The article

A fear, and a better question

The talk is built on his article, AI Won't Eat Your Brain: New Taxonomies & Opportunities for the Creative Writing Classroom. It began with a frustration. In his field, most talk about AI is about cognitive deficit: what the tool does to your brain if you engage with it too long. The advice that follows is to avoid it.

His answer was practical. For students, which includes all of us, even refusing something means engaging with it first. Otherwise we don't know what we are refusing.

Avoid, or engage first?
“What does AI do to my brain?”
“How do I engage with it well?”

Outside the lecture: the deficit worry has data behind it. An MIT Media Lab preprint (Kosmyna et al., 2025) had 54 people write essays with an LLM, a search engine or nothing, and reported weaker brain connectivity and poorer recall of their own essays in the LLM group, which the authors call “cognitive debt.” It is a small preprint about essays, not creative writing. His point is not to deny findings like this, but to ask what a classroom should do with them besides avoid.

Chapter 3 · Assumptions

Who decides what counts as progress?

Better at what?

One of his starting points: writing has always been technology. A stylus, a pencil, a surface. It is impossible to write with none, and writing always happens through mediation by the world around it.

Then he turned on the story we tell about that. The familiar picture of steady progress is a Western way of telling history, in which things are always getting better. Pick a route below and click through it.

His counter-chart runs from the bow and arrow through the musket and the gun to the atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and on to drones. The point is not that a pen is a weapon. It is that “things keep improving” hides what we chose to count.

Chapter 6 · An analogy

Walk or drive?

Speed was the row that kept bothering me, so I wrote down the picture I kept coming back to.

If I had a fast car, I could cover a mile in five minutes. On a good-weather day, I would still walk it.My analogy, tidied from my notes at the lecture

Not because the car is bad. I was not trying to arrive; I was trying to take the walk. When the destination is all that matters, or it is pouring, I will take the car.

Writing in a classroom is usually a walk. The piece is the destination, but what we are really there for is what happens to the writer on the way. Run both trips.

This is why I do not read Meekings as pro-AI or anti-AI. His slide-6 line is the same logic: identify where interventions are useful and where they are problematic. A form to fill in, a summary email: take the car. Writing as a way of thinking: take the walk, on purpose.

Chapter 8 · A connection

Disruption makes us reconsider

Look at it. With structure.

Dr. Meekings also researches writing and trauma, and the two subjects kept brushing against each other in my notes. When people work through something hard, writing helps them reconsider their own emotions: not pushing them away, but looking at them with some structure and distance.

Thinking about AI works the same way. The disruption forces us to reconsider assumptions we never examined: what counts as our writing, what effort is for, what a classroom owes a student. A reflexive commentary is the structure that makes the looking possible.

Avoiding and adopting are both ways of not looking.My reading, linking the lecture to his wider research
Chapter 9 · What changed in my question

From a verdict to a practice

“Is AI good or bad for writing?”
“Where in the process, for what purpose, and who decides?”

I came in treating it as a verdict. Here is what the lecture adds. These are practices I want to try, not claims from the talk.

From the article

Engage, then decide

Use a tool enough to know what I am accepting or refusing, and say so in a reflexive note.

From slide 5

Count what matters

Ask what the table leaves out, and do not let speed set the goals by default.

From slide 6

Choose the stage

Planning, translating, reviewing and monitoring are different acts. Decide at each one.

My question got smaller and sharper at the same time: not whether to use it, but what this particular piece of writing is for.
